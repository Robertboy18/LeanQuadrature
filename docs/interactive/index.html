01

The quantity we want

An integral, a few samples, an error.

The paper’s example asks for the integral of \(f(x)=\tfrac12(1-x)\cos x\) over \([-1,1]\). Think of the integral as the area under this particular curve. Write \(I\) for the exact answer.

\[I=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac12(1-x)\cos x\,dx=\sin 1\approx 0.8414709848.\]

The equality follows because cosine is even and \(x\cos x\) is odd: the odd part cancels across the symmetric interval. This example has a convenient exact answer, so we can see how well the numerical approximation works.

Quadrature means approximating an integral by sampling the function at a few points. Each sample point is a node. Each sample gets a weight, and we add the weighted samples. Gaussian quadrature chooses those nodes and weights to integrate many polynomials exactly.

\[Q_N(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i f(x_i).\]

Here \(N\) counts the sample points, \(x_i\) is a node, and \(w_i\) is its weight. The symbol \(Q_N\) names this finite sum. It is a different mathematical object from the integral \(I\), and it can have a different value.

Start with two nodes in the explorer. Increasing the node count changes the approximation method; it does not change the integral we are trying to approximate.

Follow the two-point calculation by hand

The two nodes are \(-1/\sqrt3\) and \(1/\sqrt3\), and both weights are one. Put \(t=1/\sqrt3\). The weighted sum simplifies to:

\[\begin{aligned}Q_2(f)&=f(-t)+f(t)\\&=\tfrac12(1+t)\cos t+\tfrac12(1-t)\cos t\\&=\cos t.\end{aligned}\]

So the integral is about \(0.841471\), while the two-point method gives about \(0.837912\). Their difference is about \(0.00355916\). This error exists even if every arithmetic operation is exact. It comes from replacing the whole curve by two samples.

A sensible specification therefore allows some error. For our certified two-point computation, we prove the bound \(0.00356\). If \(v\) is the real value represented by the returned float, the numerical part of the goal is:

\[\lvert v-I\rvert\leq 0.00356.\]

Read this as: “the returned number is within \(0.00356\) of the true integral.” The complete program claim also includes termination and valid execution. First, compare the calculation with the draft’s stated bounds. Later, the four-proof walkthrough explains how a numerical bound becomes a claim about C.

Why these particular nodes are so effective

For two nodes on \([-1,1]\), choose \(-1/\sqrt3\) and \(1/\sqrt3\) with weights 1 and 1. The rule integrates \(1,x,x^2,x^3\) exactly: their respective integrals and quadrature values are \(2,0,2/3,0\). Linearity gives exactness for every cubic.

In general, the \(N\) nodes are the roots of a monic orthogonal polynomial \(p_N\). “Monic” means its leading coefficient is one. “Orthogonal” means its integral against each lower-degree polynomial, with the given weight function, is zero.

For a polynomial \(h\) of degree at most \(2N-1\), polynomial division gives \(h=p_Nq+r\), with \(q\) and \(r\) of degree less than \(N\). Orthogonality makes the integral of \(p_Nq\) zero. Its quadrature sum is also zero, because \(p_N\) vanishes at every node. The remaining \(r\) is recovered exactly by interpolation, and the quadrature weights integrate that interpolant.

This explains the degree \(2N-1\) exactness. It does not make the rule exact for every smooth function. See the Gaussian construction in Lean ↗

03The formal remainder uses the wrong node countThe mismatch is real; \(2n+2\) is valid under the other indexing convention+

What the formal statement says. The draft’s root record contains \(n\) nodes and its sum has \(n\) terms. But the remainder lemma on page 4 uses derivative order \(2n+2\), factorial \((2n+2)!\), and the next orthogonal polynomial. For two nodes, that invokes the sixth derivative.

What the correct indexing says. With \(N\) actual nodes, the remainder uses derivative order \(2N\). Two nodes therefore require the fourth derivative. If someone instead names \(n=N-1\), the same order is \(2n+2\). That is a valid convention, and the draft uses it in its earlier mathematical exposition. The error is mixing that convention with the formal \(n\)-node definition.

Why this changes the mathematics. Take \(f(x)=x^4\) with two nodes. Its exact integral and quadrature sum differ:

\[\int_{-1}^{1}x^4\,dx-Q_2(x^4)=\frac25-\frac29=\frac8{45}\ne0,\qquad f^{(6)}(x)=0.\]

A remainder proportional to the sixth derivative would say this nonzero error is zero. The fourth derivative is \(24\), and the corrected formula gives \(24/135=8/45\), exactly as it should.

What we corrected. Our Lean development proves the counterexample and a correctly indexed remainder theorem under explicit interval, weight, and smoothness assumptions. The derivative, factorial, and nodal polynomial all refer to the same \(N\)-node rule.

The proof gap in the draft. Its remainder lemma ends with Admitted, explicitly marking it as unproved. A later Qed can certify a consequence of that assumption. It does not establish that the assumption itself is true. This finding concerns the theorem statement and its missing proof, not a demonstrated defect in Rocq or Flocq.

Read the indexing before calling it an error

The derivative is the \(2N\)-th.

The derivative in the Gaussian remainder is taken twice as many times as there are nodes. With two nodes, that means the fourth derivative. With three nodes, it means the sixth. Saying “the second derivative” for the two-node rule would be wrong.

The formula \(2n+2\) is not inherently an error. It gives the correct order when the rule has \(n+1\) nodes. The issue is whether the symbol \(n\) means the same thing throughout a theorem and the definitions it uses.

Keep \(N\) as the actual number of nodes. Then change what the letter \(n\) means. Both consistent conventions below give the same derivative order, \(2N\).

Physical rule: 2 nodesChange N in the explorer ↑
\(n=N=2\)\(2n=2\times2=4\)The fourth derivative.

When n counts the nodes, a two-node rule has n = 2. The remainder uses derivative order 2n = 4.

Rechecked in the draft and source

Where the draft mixes them

The draft’s earlier mathematical presentation uses nodes indexed 0 through \(n\), which is \(n+1\) nodes. In that convention, \(2n+2\) is the expected derivative order.

Its formal root record, however, contains ROOTS_vals: n.-tuple R: \(n\) nodes. The quadrature definition sums over indices \(i<n\). The admitted remainder lemma still uses derive1n (2*n+2) with that \(n\)-node rule.

So the correction concerns a mismatch between the formal node count and the derivative order. The paper’s \(n+1\)-node convention, considered on its own, is consistent with \(2n+2\).

The corrected general remainder formula

Assume \(a<b\), a continuous and strictly positive weight function \(w\) on \([a,b]\), and \(f\) continuously differentiable through order \(2N\) on that interval. For the \(N\)-node Gaussian rule, there is a point \(\xi\in[a,b]\) such that:

\[\int_a^b f(x)w(x)\,dx-Q_N(f)=\frac{f^{(2N)}(\xi)}{(2N)!}\int_a^b p_N(x)^2w(x)\,dx.\]

Here \(p_N(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x-x_i)\) is the monic nodal polynomial. The weight function \(w(x)\) inside the integral is distinct from the individual quadrature weights \(w_i\).

For two-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature, \(p_2(x)=x^2-\tfrac13\), and its squared integral is \(8/45\). Dividing by \(4!\) gives:

\[\lvert I-Q_2(f)\rvert\leq\frac{M}{135}\quad\text{if}\quad \lvert f^{(4)}(x)\rvert\leq M\text{ on }[-1,1].\]

The proof uses Hermite interpolation: match both function values and first derivatives at the nodes, then use repeated Rolle arguments. These repeated zeros explain the derivative order \(2N\). Read the proved remainder ↗

05

The words that need separating

One calculation, five descriptions.

Your picture of four proofs is a useful way to organize the task. There are five things to describe, with a proof connecting each neighboring pair. These are four kinds of obligation; each can require many individual lemmas.

The confusing distinction is between the numerical method, the floating-point functional model, and the C program. All three can look like “multiply the samples by their weights and add them.” They specify different details.

A

The specification: what answer counts as acceptable

The target is the real integral \(I\). The specification asks for a terminating computation whose returned value \(v\) satisfies \(\lvert v-I\rvert\leq\varepsilon\), for a stated tolerance \(\varepsilon\). For our certified two-point example, that tolerance is \(0.00356\).

This describes the result we want. It does not yet tell us how to compute it.

B

The numerical method: an ideal calculation over real numbers

We choose Gaussian quadrature. With two nodes, the ideal answer is \(Q_2=f(-1/\sqrt3)+f(1/\sqrt3)\). Here the square roots, cosine, multiplication, and addition are all exact mathematical operations.

The approximation is the replacement of the integral by this finite sum. A theorem bounding \(\lvert Q_2-I\rvert\) justifies that choice. This is the level you called the “functional numerical method.”

C

The functional model: the precise rounded calculation

A C double in this development is modeled by binary64 floating-point arithmetic. The program stores nearby representable nodes, calls an implemented approximation to the function, and rounds its arithmetic. The order of multiplication and addition matters.

We describe those operations as mathematical functions. Call the complete modeled computation \(F\). It returns a floating-point value; write \(\operatorname{value}(F)\) for the real number represented by that value. The model specifies the arithmetic and its order, without yet making claims about C pointers or memory.

“Functional model” means a mathematical function describing the computation. It may be executable inside the proof assistant. That does not, by itself, establish that a separate C program implements it.

D

The C program: arrays, calls, and a loop

The C code gets its nodes and weights from memory, calls a function, and updates a variable. Its essential structure for two samples looks like this:

double sum = 0.0;
for (int i = 0; i < 2; ++i) {
    sum = sum + weights[i] * f(nodes[i]);
}
return sum;

Schematic teaching snippet. The proofs refer to the specific Clight syntax and initialized tables linked below.

The C proof must show that these reads, calls, and iterations produce the value specified by \(F\). A correct numerical formula would not catch a C loop that stops after one sample or reads a different weight.

E

The compiled program: instructions with a formal meaning

The compiler translates the source program into lower-level instructions. Compiler correctness transfers the established source behavior to the target program under the compiler theorem’s hypotheses.

Our concrete certificates reach CompCert’s assembly semantics: assembly instructions represented inside a mathematical model of execution. Connecting that endpoint to a linked native executable and its printed output requires further steps. “Machine language” would overstate our current endpoint if it suggested those steps were already certified.

The mathematics

How close is the exact sum to the integral?

Prove a bound on |I − Q| using the smoothness of the integrand and the nodes of the rule.

Why two different error bounds are needed
\[\lvert v-I\rvert\leq\underbrace{\lvert v-Q\rvert}_{\text{rounded calculation}}+\underbrace{\lvert Q-I\rvert}_{\text{quadrature method}}.\]

The first distance measures how far the implemented arithmetic moves us from the ideal sum. The second measures how far that sum already was from the integral. The triangle inequality adds the two budgets.

For the certified two-point FloatLib example, the first term is at most \(2\times10^{-14}\); the second is about \(0.003559\). Extra floating-point precision cannot remove the error caused by choosing only two nodes.

What “the C program implements the model” actually means

Let us make the third proof concrete. Use array indices 0 and 1 for the two stored nodes. A hat marks an implemented or stored quantity: \(\widehat{x}_i\) is a stored node, \(\widehat{w}_i\) a stored weight, and \(\widehat f\) the implemented callback. A callback is the function passed to the quadrature loop to evaluate at each node.

Let \(\operatorname{mul}\) and \(\operatorname{add}\) mean the specified floating-point multiplication and addition, each with its own rounding. The functional model defines:

\[\begin{aligned}a_0&=+0,\\p_i&=\operatorname{mul}\!\bigl(\widehat w_i,\widehat f(\widehat x_i)\bigr),\\a_{i+1}&=\operatorname{add}(a_i,p_i),\qquad i=0,1,\\F&=a_2.\end{aligned}\]

The model first forms one rounded product, adds it to zero, then forms the second rounded product and adds it to the running total. Now relate that sequence to the C variable sum.

Follow the two iterationsSymbolic illustration · fixed two-node example

Before the loop, the model’s accumulator and the C variable both hold positive zero. Each iteration must preserve the connection between them.

The repeated assertion “after \(i\) samples, sum holds \(a_i\)” is a loop invariant. Prove it before the loop, prove that one iteration preserves it, and use it at the end. Then the returned C value is the model’s \(a_2\).

That argument also needs the actual array contents, valid pointer accesses, the correct loop bounds, and a callback that returns the specified values. Numerical accuracy follows only after we connect that returned value to the earlier error bound.

Where the current development splits

Why Lean and Rocq both appear.

Lean and Rocq are proof assistants. We write definitions, theorem statements, and proofs in them. Each has a proof checker, called its kernel, that checks whether the proof establishes the stated theorem from its assumptions.

They are places where we check the arrows in the previous diagram. They are not extra runtime stages between the numerical method and the C program. A theorem checked in Lean does not automatically become a theorem available to Rocq, or vice versa.

The original paper uses Rocq. Our project began from the request to formalize the mathematics with Lean libraries. The resulting repository also includes additional C and compiler proofs in Rocq, where CompCert’s execution rules and compiler theorems are available. The resulting development uses both systems; it is not all checked in Lean.

Rocq, Flocq, and the C program

Yes, the Rocq side uses Flocq. Flocq is the library for reasoning about floating-point arithmetic. C is the language of the program whose behavior we want to prove. Those names describe different parts of the verification task.

Why reusing Lean results inside Rocq still needs a connection

The direct Lean C certificates below stay within one proof assistant. A different task is to reuse a Lean numerical theorem in the existing Rocq compiler path. Use \(v_L\) for the real number represented by the Lean result and \(v_R\) for the real number represented by the Rocq result. For that cross-system task, the starting claims are:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Lean:}\quad &\lvert v_L-I\rvert\leq\varepsilon,\\ \text{Rocq:}\quad &\text{the C result represents }v_R. \end{aligned}\]

The first statement says the Lean result is accurate. The second says what the C program returns. To conclude that the C result has the Lean accuracy guarantee, the results must be connected. At the level of these real-valued conclusions, we need:

\[v_R=v_L\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lvert v_R-I\rvert=\lvert v_L-I\rvert\leq\varepsilon.\]

The substitution is straightforward once the equality is established. Establishing the connection between the actual definitions, and making the proofs compose in a checked construction, is the unfinished work. Our proposed correspondence tracks the floating-point encodings too, which is stronger than equality of their real values.

The general Lean-to-Rocq connection remains open. The direct Lean C proofs establish the execution connection in our adapted Clight semantics. They do not transfer that result to Rocq’s semantics or its compiler proof.

What each Lean library contributed

Mathlib supplies polynomial algebra, orthogonal projection, calculus, integration, and an existing Taylor–Lagrange theorem. LeanPDE supplies the normalized quadrature interface, the explicit two-point Gauss–Legendre rule, cubic exactness, stability estimates, symbolic integration, and enclosure tools. It also already contains the sharp fourth-derivative error bound for the two-point rule, proved using a Peano kernel. FloatLib supplies binary64 semantics and the cosine machinery used in the Lean certificates.

LeanQuadrature adds the general compact-interval proofs: the orthogonal family, its recurrence and roots, Gaussian construction, positive weights, exactness, the multi-node Hermite and integrated remainders, and convergence as the order increases. The 24-paragraph comparison identifies the exact declarations and hypotheses. Some textbook arguments are bypassed by a different proof; the two infinite-interval families remain outside this development.

The remainder proof constructs a polynomial matching values and first derivatives at each node. For the integrated identity, the integrand is ContDiffOn through order \(2n\) on the closed interval, and the result uses derivatives within that interval. This is not an interpolant API for arbitrary derivative multiplicities.

TorchLean is present through the pinned dependencies, but this scalar quadrature development does not use its tensor API. These libraries do not automatically provide this project’s C-to-FloatLib refinement theorem.

LeanPDE is private. Contact Robert to arrange access before running the full build. The public repository and source archive contain this project, without the private dependency.

The general construction assumes a finite interval and a continuous weight strictly positive on the closed interval. Convergence concerns exact-real rules; it does not promise unlimited accuracy from fixed binary64 arithmetic. Hermite interpolation in the remainder proof is different from infinite-interval Gauss–Hermite quadrature.

General root existence and convergence extend the original draft’s chosen formal scope. Their earlier absence is not an author mistake. Verifying this quadrature component also does not verify a whole PDE or finite-element solver.

For a conversation with the authors, this describes the work without overstating it:

We formalized finite-interval Gaussian quadrature in Lean and checked four specific issues in the original development. Lean checks the selected C functions and tables, together with a verified C polynomial that replaces the external cosine. From initialized memory, the original integrator and wrapper return the certified FloatLib values without table or cosine assumptions. Every permitted evaluation order terminates with that result in our adaptation of CompCert’s C small-step semantics. For the six selected functions, Lean also proves that Clight normalization preserves returned values, traces, termination, and caller memory. Separately, Lean proves all ten imported polynomial applications through formal assembly and relates their final observations to authored Clight callers. Each result has a certified integral-error bound. General-integrand table certificates cover all ten orders under explicit numerical hypotheses. Independent Rocq compiler certificates also reach formal assembly for the ten polynomial applications. The remaining work includes general parser and elaborator correctness, correspondence between the Lean and Rocq representations and models, a linked executable, and infinite-interval quadrature.

The recorded result is a formal Clight annotation. Its exact value is proved; decimal printing and terminal output remain separate work. The independent Rocq compiler proofs have not been transferred into Lean.
